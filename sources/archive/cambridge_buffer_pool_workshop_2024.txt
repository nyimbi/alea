                      Historical Context
                      Carbon markets and emissions trading systems seek to leverage markets to incentivize
                      and mobilize finance for cost-effective, standardized and verified greenhouse gas
                      emission reductions and carbon removals. Carbon markets broadly refer to “cap and
                      trade” and other emissions trading systems, as well as voluntary and compliance
                      “offset” markets that generate tradeable certificates representing one tonne of CO2e
                      avoided, reduced, or removed from the atmosphere.
                      Demand for these certificates is driven by either 1) compliance obligations on
                      companies or governments as a result of regulation or 2) voluntary actors seeking to
                      deliver on climate action goals or targets.
                      Conventions and terminology have changed over the years but generally speaking, a
                      “carbon credit” represents one metric tonne of CO2e avoided, reduced or removed
                      emissions from the atmosphere. Most carbon credits are used as a carbon “offset”, e.g.
                      to compensate for a tonne of emissions elsewhere, either under mandatory or voluntary
                      systems. If a carbon credit is claimed as an offset, there’s a general expectation in the
                      market that purchasing and retiring a carbon offset delivers an equivalent or
                      commensurate amount of atmospheric benefit/climate change mitigation benefit to the
                      tonne of emissions you are compensating for.
                      The concept of “permanence” has long been considered a key tenant of a high-quality
                      carbon credit. “Permanence” is the concept that carbon removed or reduced through an
                      offset project must have a high degree of certainty that it will last for a sufficient period
                      of time to account for/compensate for the harm of the impact of carbon dioxide in the
                      atmosphere it is intended to address.
                      However, to date, carbon credits considered “permanent” have been issued from a
                      range of project types resulting in carbon removed or reduced for different periods of
                      time—ranging from a year to centuries. This lack of consistent accounting for
                      differences in storage or reduction duration across project types, and the lack of
                      consensus across the policy making and scientific fields about the appropriate period of
                      time required to store carbon in order to fully mitigate the negative impacts of the
                      compensated tonne, has led to concerns over the validity of some claims based on
                      carbon credits and uncertainty around how to value the climate impact from different
                      projects of different lengths. Despite broad recognition of the atmospheric value and
                      benefit of temporary removal and storage of carbon, there is not yet consensus on how
                      to value these types of projects relative to projects that store carbon for much longer
                      terms.
                      Recently, “durability” has gained traction as an alternative to “permanence.” This
                      represents a recognition that shorter term solutions have value in climate mitigation and
                      that a range of approaches are available to value and conduct accounting for the
                      duration of impact (i.e., minimizing the risk that the impact is reversed or not achieved
                      for the length of time we initially believe it to be) rather than a minimum time period that
                      all projects must adhere to. Others in the market insist that only solutions that last as
                      long as carbon in the atmosphere (i.e., between 300 and 1,000 years) have merit.

                      Current approaches to permanence across voluntary and compliance standard setting
                      bodies vary widely with respect to classification of risk across mitigation project types,
                      how removals and reductions are accounted for, risk mitigation and management
                      assessments and systems, length of monitoring, reporting and verification periods, etc.
                      This is a key policy issue but has historically been driven by individual standard bodies
                      and mechanisms and addressed piecemeal across the market. Notably California has
                      extended the disclosure requirements to durability and risk mitigation and specified
                      these be “for any project that the business entity knows or should know that such
                      reductions or removal enhancements are less than the atmospheric lifetime of carbon
                      dioxide emissions.”
                      Incumbent ratings entities, data providers, insurers and price assessment agencies
                      evaluate duration without a common framework/s to compare projects of different
                      durations. Rather, proxies are used for data such as credit type and vintage.
                      The European emission trading scheme appears to favor a conservative approach to
                      equivalence for VCCs and exclusion of any carbon credits, even if permanent. A recent
                      CO2RE report called for the focus on monitoring reporting and verification be applied to
                      all removals ‘regardless of whether of differing permanence and policy/market design.
                      Although the UNFCCC Supervisory Body for Article 6.4 decided in 2023 to ‘focus on

                      carbon accounting method (tonne-year carbon accounting method discussed below)
                      from near-term considerations’ work is ongoing and Article 6.4 agreements pushed out

                      Permanence and Durability Approaches
                      Approaches to address permanence and durability risks in the voluntary carbon market
                      range from wholesale changes to the way carbon storage is accounted for to more
                      targeted risk mitigation measures that can be aligned to most existing standards bodies
                      today. Below is an overview of sample approaches to address permanence and
                      durability in the voluntary markets that may be discussed more in-depth at the
                      workshop.
                      The descriptions below do not include commentary on the market demand for such
                      approaches but focus on how they address permanence or durability risk in the supply
                      of carbon credits. This is not to say that demand is not important. On the contrary,
                      market demand is a vital consideration when determining the feasibility of implementing
                      any given approach along with other considerations (see Permanence and Durability
                      Parameters). Furthermore, how the “demand” side of the market, e.g. carbon credit
                      purchasers and investors, sees requirements imposed by standard setting bodies and
                      regulators has a significant impact on whether a given approach to permanence will be
                      successful over time. Feasibility of application and acceptability by “demand” side
                      actors should be taken into consideration.
                      IC-VCM, despite its focus on the supply side, regularly coordinates with other
                      ecosystem actors more focused on the demand side of the market, including the
                      Voluntary Carbon Markets Initiative, We Mean Business Coalition, Climate Disclosure

                      Supply Side Targeted Risk Mitigation Mechanisms
                      These approaches to address permanence and durability risks are focused specifically
                      on the supply side of the market and involve little change in how buyers would account
                      for their emissions.
                      Minimum Project Duration with Monitoring, Reporting, and Verification (MRV),
                      Compensation, and Liability Assignment
                      It is common practice in the carbon crediting markets to require a monitoring, reporting,
                      and verification (MRV) and compensation period during which project developers must
                      regularly monitor for any reversals and implement mitigation or compensation measures
                      if the emissions underlying a previously issued credit are reversed.
                      Each standard takes a different approach to determining the length of their MRV and
                      compensation period, typically based on a combination of project type risk and
                      individual project risk assessments. For example, Climate Action Reserve’s U.S. Forest
                      Protocol specifies that forest projects must be monitored and verified “for a period of
                      100 years following the issuance of any [climate reserve tonne] for GHG reductions or
                      removals achieved by the project.” Verra requires its agriculture, forestry, and other land
                      use projects to assess permanence risk over 100 years; however, it requires a minimum
                      project duration of only 40 years. To account for this difference, Verra is developing a
                      long-term monitoring system for projects certified under its methodologies that would
                      continue to monitor reversal risk after the crediting period or MRV and compensation
                      period expires.
                      MRV and compensation periods for engineered removals vary depending on project
                      type. For example, geologic storage protocols often point to the U.S. Environmental
                      Protection Agency’s guidance around class VI wells for carbon dioxide sequestration,
                      which requires a minimum 50-year monitoring period after the injection well is closed.
                      That being said, while government regulations may require an MRV period, protocols
                      may not require compensation in the event of a reversal for engineered solutions—even
                      during this MRV period.
                      In addressing permanence and durability risk in its Assessment Framework, IC-VCM
                      provided guidance around MRV and compensation periods by activity type. For
                      activities that are considered to have a “material risk” of reversal (i.e., conservation and

                      Buffer Pools
                      Buffer pools are the most commonly used tool to address permanence and durability
                      risks in the market today and were specifically addressed by IC-VCM in its Assessment
                      Framework. Buffer pools are a mechanism whereby a portion of a project’s overall
                      credits are reserved to cover unplanned reversals and ensure that any specific entity
                      (whether a project or a credit issuer or otherwise) has reduced or removed more carbon
                      dioxide from the atmosphere than it has released. Buffer pools are used by the largest
                      registries today (e.g., Verra, Gold Standard, American Climate Registry, Climate Action
                      Reserve, etc.) and are typically managed by the registries themselves; however, each
                      standard takes a slightly different approach to buffer pool management.
                      In its Assessment Framework, IC-VCM provided guidance for when buffer pools are
                      required and how they should be structured. For projects that are considered to have a
                      material risk of reversal (i.e., conservation and avoided conversion, agriculture soil
                      carbon sequestration, forestry sequestration, and wetland and marine ecosystem
                      restoration/management), IC-VCM requires a minimum of 20% of all credits issued by
                      the standard to be placed in a pooled buffer reserve. For projects that are considered to
                      have a lower risk of reversal (i.e., displacement of non-renewable biomass, biochar,
                      carbon capture with geological storage or mineralization, enhanced weathering, carbon
                      dioxide in concrete) IC-VCM requires standards to conduct a general risk assessment
                      and implement appropriate measures to mitigate any material risks. For Jurisdictional
                      REDD+ projects, IC-VCM requires a pooled buffer reserve but leaves discretion on the
                      adequate size, noting that it should be “proportional to reversal risk and adequate to
                      compensate for potential reversals.”
                      In the next iteration of its Assessment Framework, IC-VCM noted that it would consider
                      requiring credit issuers to have provisions in place to ensure the continued operation of
                      pooled buffer reserves until the latest expiration of any given project (e.g., in case the
                      credit issuer ceases to exist or can no longer operate the pooled buffer reserve).

                      While some consider buffer pools a form of insurance, other third-party insurance
                      offerings have begun to appear. Insurance policies targeted at the voluntary carbon
                      market are being offered by large more traditional insurers and startups alike; however,
                      policies that cover risk related to permanence and durability remain few and far
                      between. Several insurers have chosen to enter the market focused on mitigating other
                      risks associated with carbon credit transactions, including political risk and delivery risk,
                      among others.
                      Insurance policies that address permanence and durability could be targeted at project
                      developers, carbon credit buyers, and credit issuers. For project developers, an
                      insurance policy could cover an unavoidable reversal after credits have been issued
                      (e.g., an afforestation project damaged by wildfire or pest infestation or a carbon dioxide
                      injection site with a caprock damaged by unrelated seismic activity). For carbon credit
                      buyers, an insurance policy could cover a portfolio of credits purchased and be priced
                      according to the activities associated with the credits purchased. For credit issuers, an
                      insurance policy could cover the pooled buffer reserve, providing issuers with the ability
                      to compensate those who purchased the credits.
                      An open question among insurers related to permanence and durability is that of
                      fungibility. Given that buyers are often purchasing a carbon credit to offset an emission,
                      monetary compensation may not be the preferred remedy. Instead, insurers are
                      exploring the possibility of paying out policies in carbon credits, but this introduces a
                      new set of issues to be addressed—should the replacement credit be from the same
                      activity? Should it be from the same issuer? Should the replacement credits be from a
                      project in the same geography? What are the ecological impacts associated with the
                      different options? These questions and more are still being debated, with insurers
                      developing their own approaches to fungibility.
                      Discounting
                      Discounting is another approach that is used to account for permanence and durability
                      risk as well as other risk factors associated with a carbon credit. There are both ex-post
                      and ex-ante examples of discounting being applied to carbon credits. It is common
                      practice for crediting methodologies and standards to require a variety of discounts to
                      be applied prior to credit issuance to address a range of issues, including uncertainty
                      and to ensure conservativeness. In some instances buyers and developers have
                      applied an ex post credit discount, where a credit that is issued as equal to a metric
                      tonne of carbon dioxide removed from the atmosphere is multiplied by a percentage
                      somewhere between 0% and 100% to become worth less than one metric tonne of
                      carbon dioxide removed according to its individual risk profile as determined by the
                      buyer or broker. This method is extended to the project level, where the overall volume
                      of issued credits is discounted to account for permanence, durability risk, and other
                      associated risks.
                      Risk-adjusted discounting is commonly used in financial markets and has its origins in
                      the capital asset pricing model first published in 1964 and is still frequently used to
                      value future cash flows. While risk-adjusted discounting has been a concept discussed
                      in the carbon markets since shortly after the adoption of the Kyoto Protocol, it has more
                      recently been a feature of how carbon credit ratings agencies assign value to a carbon

                      Other activity and methodology-specific mechanisms
                      There are several activity-specific mechanisms that could be implemented at the project
                      level to aid in mitigating permanence or durability risk. Each of these mechanisms will
                      be specific to the particular activity type and are typically addressed through project
                      specific methodology requirements.
                      For example, in its geologically stored carbon methodology, Puro.Earth requires that
                      carbon dioxide be sequestered in reservoirs or geological formations that meet U.S.
                      Environmental Protection Agency class I, class II, class VI, or EU regulations. The Gold
                      Standard, requires that, with limited exceptions, calcium carbonate from indirect mineral
                      carbonation must be used as filler in construction materials only to avoid accidental
                      release through municipal solid waste incineration. Verra recently concluded a public
                      comment period for its proposed ABACUS label for afforestation, reforestation, and
                      revegetation activities, outlining several project-level measures to enhance permanence
                      and durability. The project working group proposed mandating that projects be
                      “ecologically appropriate” for the geographic area, disallowing projects with more than
                      one contiguous hectare of monoculture, and requiring at the project validation phase a
                      proposed approach to ensure carbon stock stability after the crediting period (e.g., legal
                      protection, ongoing capacity building, alignment of financial incentives, etc.).
                      Different Approaches to Account for Carbon Emissions
                      These approaches differ from those outlined above in that they could require a change
                      in how purchasers approach carbon credit use and accounting to address permanence
                      and durability risk in carbon credit projects.
                      Tonne-Year Accounting
                      Tonne-year accounting refers to a family of approaches that seek to introduce time into
                      how carbon emissions are accounted for, providing market participants with a common
                      unit (a tonne-year) and method to compare the impact and durability of different credited
                      emission mitigation activities with the impact and duration of emissions from elsewhere
                      in the economy. In the voluntary carbon markets, the application most associated with
                      tonne-year accounting is known as vertical stacking.
                      Vertical stacking refers to “buying multiple short-termne-year accounting aims to provide
                      a means to account for and compare the impact of temporary carbon storage and
                      projects of different durations. ” credits upfront to “offset one tonne of carbon dioxide
                      emitted today.” For example, to offset one agetonne emitted in 2020, an organization
                      would purchase multiple credits with a 20-year duration to attempt to approximate the

                      vertical stacking is determining how high the stack should be—e.g., is it two 20-year
                      credits? Three? More? Two prominent approaches for calculating the value of
                      temporary storage in a vertical stack, Moura-Costa and Lashof, were first introduced in
                      the 1990s, while more recent research has sought to reframe the conversation around
                      the social value of an offset.
                      While the IC-VCM did not explicitly address tonne-year accounting in its Assessment
                      Framework, it noted during its feedback summary that tonne-year accounting
                      approaches without any minimum time commitment would not be granted Core Carbon
                      Principles status. The expert panel acknowledged that tonne-year accounting in some
                      form “could work as an incentive mechanism or policy tool for maintaining carbon
                      storage;” however, it rejected the “flawed premise that storing carbon for arbitrarily short
                      periods of time” could be deemed equal to a permanent reduction in emissions.
                      Temporary Crediting
                      Often linked with tonne-year accounting, but arguably a distinct concept is the idea of
                      temporary crediting. Temporary crediting frequently relies on some of the same
                      scholarship (i.e., Moura-Costa, Lashof, Groom and Venmans) in seeking to arrive at a
                      value for short-term carbon storage and then allows for a credit “expiration” after a given
                      time interval. What happens upon expiration is still up for debate—carbon credit buyers
                      could then replace the credit with another one, a public entity could manage the
                      replacement of expiring credits, etc.—the key element is just that the credit itself is time-
                      bound.
                      Temporary crediting could have positive atmospheric and social benefits. In terms of
                      atmospheric benefits, it could give “ecosystems more time to adapt to climate change”
                      and also provide society with more time to “develop and deploy alternative mitigation
                      actions.” From a social perspective, temporary crediting could direct capital to
                      indigenous peoples and local communities who are often intimately involved in projects
                      that issue temporary credits (e.g., reforestation and ecosystem restoration projects).
                      Permanent Additional Carbon Tonne
                      The Permanent Additional Carbon Tonne (PACT) is a framework for quantifying the
                      long-term economic value of nature-based solutions to compare directly with carbon
                      capture and storage or geosequestration. It was developed by a team of Cambridge
                      University researchers and first published in October 2023. The framework includes six
                      elements: additionality, permanence, leakage, biodiversity, livelihoods, and justice.

                      Current approaches to permanence and durability vary among carbon crediting
                      programs and activity type. With the release of the IC-VCM’s Core Carbon Principles

                      IC-VCM standards, while others were already meeting or exceeding IC-VCM guidance.

                      American Carbon Registry
                      The American Carbon Registry’s reversal risk mitigation requirements are specific to the
                      activity type, with AFOLU projects mirroring IC-VCM’s 40-year monitoring and
                      compensation period requirement. Across both AFOLU and CCS projects, ACR requires
                      project developers to contribute to a pooled buffer reserve that can be used as
                      compensation in the event of a reversal risk. The contribution from AFOLU projects is
                      determined based on a risk assessment, with lower risk factors leading to a lower
                      required contribution. For CCS projects, the requirement is a flat 10% with a required
                      binding risk mitigation covenant. No information regarding contingency plans for the
                      continued operation of the pooled buffer reserve was found.
                      Architecture for REDD+ Transactions (ART)
                      The ART TREES program requires a reversal risk assessment to determine a buffer
                      contribution that is tailored to the individual jurisdiction. Reversal risk is evaluated every
                      five years; however, should a jurisdiction withdraw from the program, no further
                      monitoring related to already issued credits is required. No information regarding
                      contingency plans for the continued operation of the pooled buffer reserve was found.
                      Climate Action Reserve
                      Both of Climate Action Reserve’s protocols require a reversal risk assessment to
                      determine the required buffer contribution, with the contribution lower if more risk
                      mitigation measures are included in the project design. Climate Action Reserve
                      indicates that its bylaws require the continued operation of the pooled buffer reserve
                      should the organization cease to exist, but the exact pathway is left undetermined and
                      dependent on future market conditions. The program may publish information regarding

                      The Gold Standard
                      Unlike other carbon crediting programs, Gold Standard requires a flat 20% contribution
                      to its pooled buffer reserve by all land-use projects, with other built-in requirements that
                      seek to minimize reversal risk. Gold Standard was also one of the few standards to set
                      a maximum project duration period for land-use projects. No information regarding
                      contingency plans for the continued operation of the pooled buffer reserve was found.
                      Isometric
                      Isometric requires a small, 2% contribution to a pooled buffer reserve as a reversal risk
                      mitigation measure, even though the program focuses on methodologies that achieve
                      “effective” permanence. The program requires other risk mitigation measures aligned to
                      state or national regulations for its saline aquifer storage module. No information
                      regarding contingency plans for the continued operation of the pooled buffer reserve or
                      information on the pooled buffer reserve’s contents was found.
                      Plan Vivo
                      Plan Vivo requires a flat 20% of all issued credits to be transferred to its risk buffer and
                      requires mitigation of significant reversal risks for at least 50 years. Plan Vivo’s standard
                      also incorporates several other requirements aimed at further mitigating reversal risk.
                      Plan Vivo has published the number of credits in its pooled buffer reserve for previous
                      protocols; however, it has not updated its website with information for the most recent
                      versions. No information regarding contingency plans for the continued operation of the
                      pooled buffer reserve was found.
                      Puro.Earth
                      Puro.Earth was the only carbon crediting program researched that does not have a
                      pooled buffer reserve to manage reversal risk. Instead, it relies on a combination of
                      monitoring requirements, documentation requirements (e.g., land title in the case of
                      terrestrial biomass storage) and pre-issuance deductions to manage reversal risk.
                      Verra
                      Verra recently updated some of its guidance to mirror the permanence requirements
                      contained in the IC-VCM’s Assessment Framework. Verra is the only standard that is
                      building a long-term monitoring capability that will eventually be deployed to monitor for
                      reversal risk after the project period concludes. Verra requires that every program
                      conduct a risk assessment to determine a buffer pool contribution, with a lower
                      contribution tied to strong risk mitigation measures. Of all carbon crediting programs
                      investigated, Verra provided the most comprehensive account of its pooled buffer
                      reserve contents, with data down to the project level publicly available. No information
                      regarding contingency plans for the continued operation of the pooled buffer reserve
                      was found.

                      IC-VCM Glossary
                      The glossary below was developed by the IC-VCM and released in tandem with their
                      Core Carbon Principles and Assessment Framework.1
                      Baseline Scenario
                      A description of the situation and the outcome that is predicted or assumed to occur in
                      the absence of the incentives created by the carbon credits and their associated
                      mitigation activities, while holding all other factors constant.
                      Benefit Distribution Mechanism(s)
                      The system(s) or channel(s) through which Monetary and/or Non-Monetary Benefits are
                      distributed.
                      Benefit Sharing
                      The sharing of Monetary and Non-Monetary Benefits with mitigation activity’s Partners.
                      Benefit Sharing Arrangement(s)
                      The arrangement(s) that describe Partners, Monetary and Non-Monetary Benefits, and
                      the Benefit Distribution Mechanism(s). Benefit Sharing Arrangements describe the
                      processes for the distribution of Monetary and Non-Monetary Benefits to Beneficiaries,
                      including the types and proportions of benefits to be shared and the Mechanism by
                      which such benefits will be distributed. Benefit Sharing Arrangements are included in a
                      part of mitigation activity documentation and form the basis of the development of the
                      Benefit Sharing Plan.
                      Carbon Credit
                      A tradable financial instrument that is issued by a carbon-crediting program. A carbon
                      credit represents a greenhouse gas emission reduction to, or removal from, the
                      atmosphere equivalent to one metric tonne of carbon dioxide equivalent, calculated as
                      the difference in emissions from a baseline scenario to a project scenario. Carbon
                      credits are uniquely serialized, issued, tracked and retired or administratively cancelled
                      by means of an electronic registry operated by an administrative body, such as a
                      carbon-crediting program.
                      Carbon-Crediting Program
                      A standard setting program that registers mitigation activities and issues carbon credits.
                      Conservative Approaches
                      A conservative approach errs on the side of caution to ensure emission reductions or
                      removals are unlikely to be overestimated.
                      Crediting Period
                      The period in which verified emission reductions or removals attributable to a mitigation
                      activity can result in the issuance of carbon credits.
                      Double Claiming
                      A type of double counting in which the same emission reduction or removal is claimed

                      Workshop Overview
                      On January 31 and February 1, 2024, 60+ experts from across the carbon market
                      ecosystem—policymakers, practitioners, academia, and more—convened at Cambridge
                      University to discuss issues and challenges related to permanence and durability and
                      identify potential solutions for the continued development of the market.
                      The discussion at the Cambridge Permanence and Durability Workshop will serve as an
                      input to the IC-VCM CIWP on Permanence. After publishing its Core Carbon Principles
                      and corresponding Assessment Framework in July 2023, the IC-VCM launched the
                      CIWPs to consider key issues related to the future of the carbon markets and to develop
                      recommendations to inform the next iteration of IC-VCM CCP label requirements.
                      The Cambridge Permanence and Durability Workshop was the first step of a rigorous
                      stakeholder engagement process leading up to the release of the revised IC-VCM
                      standards in 2026.
                      The workshop was designed with two objectives in mind:
                                 Develop a shared understanding among the expert community on 1)
                                  approaches to addressing the issue of permanence for carbon crediting
                                  purposes, and 2) on key open questions or gaps in understanding or knowledge.
                                 Develop recommendations for addressing permanence (including risk
                                  assessment, allocation, and mitigation) that will result in “investment-grade”
                                  carbon credits that maintain scientific rigor in delivering climate impact
                                  while balancing complex policy, legal, financial, equity, and implementation
                                  tradeoffs.
                      These objectives tie directly to the IC-VCM’s overarching mission of setting definitive
                      global standards using the best available science and expertise, so that high-quality
                      carbon credits can serve as a financing mechanism for genuine and additional
                      greenhouse gas (GHG) reductions and removals.
                      In line with these objectives and the IC-VCM mission, the workshop had two distinct
                      parts: 1) expert presentation and discussions related to permanence and durability and
                      2) a “systems design” exercise. The panel discussions addressed a range of topics
                      related to permanence, including scientific perspectives on the economic and
                      atmospheric impact and value of carbon storage, to the role of the financial sector and
                      insurance in carbon markets, to an overview of how standard-setting bodies currently
                      address permanence and durability. During the systems design exercise, workshop
                      participants worked in small groups to develop recommendations for how permanence
                      and durability should be addressed by various market actors.

                      The Cambridge Workshop provided a forum to share perspectives on permanence and
                      durability in carbon markets. Ahead of the workshop, the organizers conducted informal,
                      30-minute interviews with 30+ attendees to solicit their opinion on the unresolved
                      challenges regarding permanence and durability, promising measures to mitigate
                      reversal risk, and the role of the IC-VCM in issuing permanence and durability guidance

                      opportunity to share their reflections in small-group and large-group formats.
                      As expected, attendees’ views differed depending on their role in the ecosystem and
                      beliefs about the role of carbon credits in driving climate benefits; however, workshop
                      participants noted that there appeared to be more consensus than previous discussions
                      about permanence and durability. Below is an aggregated, anonymized synthesis of
                      views about key topics expressed during pre-workshop interviews and discussion during
                      the Workshop itself.
                      Shift From Permanence to Durability
                      In the pre-workshop interviews, participants were quick to point out the importance of
                      semantics when discussing reversal risk, noticing the unique name of the workshop:
                      The Cambridge Permanence and Durability Workshop. While “permanence” has
                      historically been the accepted term for this type of risk, several attendees advocated for
                      shifting to a discussion of durability as a better way to conceptualize the issue. This
                      change in language reflects that there are several different factors that determine how
                      long the benefits of a particular mitigation activity can be expected—some of which are
                      easier to control for than others—and that there is an inherent unknown when
                      discussing the length of time that an activity sequesters carbon. As one participant
                      noted in their reflections, “what’s important is that carbon stays sequestered for as long
                      as it matters.”
                      For projects deemed to have a material risk of reversal, the IC-VCM currently sets that
                      threshold at 40 years, but in a panel with members of carbon crediting programs, it was
                      evident that each body takes a different approach (see Current Practice Among Carbon
                      Crediting Programs). Throughout this panel discussion and the workshop, the ideal
                      minimum threshold to establish “permanence” was debated, with participants voicing
                      support for anything from 20 years to 100+ years. Notably, there were no participants
                      that advocated for a 1,000-year permanence standard, even though there are market
                      proponents of this approach.2 Vocal opposition towards longer thresholds came from
                      the project developer community, noting that in many parts of the world (especially the
                      Global South), there is no legal framework in place or appetite among landowners to

                      Issue of Fungibility
                      In the pre-workshop interviews, nearly every interviewee raised the issue of fungibility
                      (i.e., equating one tonne of carbon from one project to a tonne of carbon from a different
                      project or emissions source with a different duration) as one of the top open questions
                      around permanence and durability. There remains no market-wide consensus on how
                      purchasers or regulatory bodies should address varying levels of reversal risk or
                      duration associated with credits from different activity types.
                      Some participants argued that once minimum reversal risk mitigation measures were in
                      place (e.g., a buffer pool, insurance, etc.), durability should not matter to carbon credit
                      purchasers, assuming the credit is backed by a credible system that “makes the
                      atmosphere whole” in the event of a rerelease of stored carbon. Their rationale was
                      rooted in the severity of the climate emergency that we are now facing, given slow and
                      insufficient progress against the 1.5C° threshold agreed to under the Paris Agreement. 4

                      is climate benefit that accrues at most durations of carbon sequestration. If society has
                      the privilege of debating permanence in 50 years, they argue, then we have averted the
                      worst of the anticipated climate disasters. These participants often highlighted that it is
                      impossible to guarantee the permanence of any carbon removal or reduction outcome,
                      given that legal and market structures do not exist to ensure longevity beyond several
                      decades, and there is significant uncertainty related to climate or human conditions in

                      Improved Compensation Mechanisms
                      In the most recent Assessment Framework, the IC-VCM endorsed buffer pools as the
                      sole compensation mechanism for avoidable reversal risks, as it was the only approach
                      that had gained widespread market acceptance. It also noted that there are a range of
                      additional reversal compensation mechanisms under development and piloting across
                      the market, including insurance, and that those will be further explored in the context of
                      the Continuous Improvement Work Programs. Workshop participants agreed on the
                      need to improve buffer pool design and operation, while also allowing for the use of
                      alternative compensation mechanisms.
                      Workshop attendees linked three principal issues to buffer pools when discussing their
                      improvement: risk allocation, appropriate capitalization, and conflict of interest. The
                      issue of risk allocation centers on which parties bear the risk of reversal when. Current
                      IC-VCM guidance places this risk largely on the carbon crediting program or project
                      developer for a defined monitoring and compensation period, after which no one bears
                      the risk. Workshop participants suggested ideas for how risk could be redistributed by
                      requiring purchasers to bear some risk and replace credits that face reversal, creating a
                      public entity to take on the risk after the monitoring and compensation period ends, or
                      simply improving current buffer pool management with “sunset clauses” to transfer risk
                      after a certain period of time or when the organization managing the buffer ceases to
                      exist. Standard setting bodies are also exploring whether long-term liability for
                      monitoring and reversal mitigation could shift to them from the developer at the end of a
                      project’s active crediting period. Most participants agreed that project developers or
                      carbon crediting programs should continue to bear the costs of reversal risk, with one
                      participant noting that it is unheard of that a buyer should be responsible for the quality
                      of a product in other industries.

                      that insurance is not a panacea and that the industry is still nascent. While some of the
                      larger insurance conglomerates participate in the space, insurance for carbon credits is
                      still largely specialized, and insurance for reversal risk in particular is still developing.

                      analysis of the buffer pool associated with California’s cap-and-trade program found that

                      pair with issuances for shorter-duration activity types, requiring the use of customized
                      risk assessments instead of blanket buffer pool contributions for high-risk mitigation
                      activities, or insurance policies for pooled buffer reserves that payout when the buffer
                      drops below a certain credit count.

                      buffer pools: those who manage buffer pools as a risk mitigation mechanism are also
                      responsible for emitting carbon credits. In a competitive marketplace for carbon
                      crediting programs, standards bodies could be incentivized to lower the amount of
                      buffer contributions required to allow project developers to issue more credits and
                      maximize their revenue. Carbon crediting programs to-date have built in safeguards
                      between those who may interface with project developers and those who manage the
                      risk evaluation process; however, given they are affiliated with the same organization,
                      the risk cannot be mitigated entirely.

                      Systems Design Exercise Outputs
                      The systems design exercise provided space for workshop participants to work in small
                      groups of diverse backgrounds to develop a system that would effectively address
                      permanence and durability risk while achieving three objectives: delivering climate
                      change impact, maintaining an appropriate quality bar for high-integrity supply of carbon
                      credits, and encouraging investment at a scale commensurate with the size of the
                      climate crisis. Each group was asked to describe any risk mitigation and
                      or/compensation mechanisms envisioned in their system, consider the benefits and
                      tradeoffs of adopting the approach, outline any considerations for specific project types,
                      and evaluate their system against the Permanence and Durability Parameters. In total,
                      there were eight different groups, each with their own unique perspective; however,
                      several reversal risk mitigation mechanisms appeared across groups.
                      Common Threads Throughout Outputs
                      Adjusting buffer pool requirements to focus on more durable solutions. Three
                      groups mentioned changing buffer pool requirements at the carbon crediting program
                      level over time to focus on solutions with less reversal risk. For example, a given
                      standard or registry would be required to have a minimum percentage (e.g., 15%) of its
                      pooled buffer reserve comprised of credits from low-reversal risk credits (e.g., carbon
                      capture with geologic storage) and this percentage would increase over time until 100%
                      of the pooled buffer reserve was made up of these credits. Adopting this guidance
                      would likely require a change in how carbon crediting programs manage their pooled
                      buffer reserve. Currently, the pooled buffer reserve makeup generally mirrors the
                      portfolio of projects certified by a particular standard. This guidance could see carbon
                      crediting programs work with project developers to contribute credits to the pooled
                      buffer reserve that are not necessarily credits from the program in which they are
                      certifying, depending on the threshold of low-reversal risk credits required.
                      Contributing to a fund for long-duration credits. A couple of the groups
                      recommended the creation of a fund to invest in long-duration credits. The fund could
                      be capitalized in a variety of ways, including through a potential fee levied on the
                      issuance of short-duration credits or through required contributions to the fund of shorter
                      duration credits that could be liquidated in the event of a reversal and proceeds used to
                      procure permanent CDR. These funds could then be invested in a trust or endowment
                      to compensate for reversals after the conclusion of the 40-year monitoring and
                      compensation period, with a focus on supporting the development of engineered
                      solutions that have a longer duration. Participants noted that this idea recognizes the
                      risks associated with shorter-duration projects and could provide a source of capital to
                      build the capacity of longer-duration projects over time.

                      Assessment Framework, the IC-VCM set a minimum 40-year monitoring and
                      compensation period for projects deemed to have high reversal risk. Several groups
                      aligned around a 100-year monitoring and compensation period as a revised minimum
                      duration requirement; however, the risk allocation and responsibilities could change
                      throughout the period. For example, several groups suggested that project developers
