        11     ABSTRACT
        12     Water and energy are interdependent on each other. Energy is required to supply water to a
        13     system while, at the same time, water is needed for power generation in any natural or artificial
        14     system. This relationship is often called the Water-Energy-Nexus (WEN). In a water supply
        15     system, energy is consumed for source water extraction, transmission, treatment, and
        16     distribution. About 7-8% of the world’s total generated energy is used for drinking water
        17     production and distribution. A major portion of this energy is used for distribution, i.e., pumping,
        18     chlorination, and maintenance activities, and hence is the focus of this review. Most of the
        19
        20
                                                                   Dr
               world’s energy is generated by fossil fuels (oil, gas, and coal), which results in greenhouse gas
               (GHG) emissions. Here we review studies conducted to assess and evaluate the energy
        21
        22                                                                  aft
               consumption and the related GHG emissions in water distribution systems (WDSs). This review
               covers the basic concepts and studies on WEN, energy saving solutions, renewable energy
        23     resources for water pumping, optimization of design, and the life cycle assessment (LCA) of
        24     large WDSs. Most of the reviewed studies suggest a trade-off between energy cost and the
        25     associated GHG emissions when selecting fixed speed pumps over variable speed pumps for
        26     large WDSs. To mitigate CO2 emissions, renewable energy resources like solar, wind and mini-
        27     water turbines for water pumping have been discussed and mini-water turbines were found to be
        28     energy efficient solutions. The energy focused LCA model has been studied to investigate the
        29     environmental impacts, GHG emissions, operational energy, and various life cycle stages of pipe
        30     manufacturing (embodied energy) in the network. Case studies of real world WDSs are reviewed
        31     and the potential research gaps are identified. Most life cycle studies have focused on the areas
        32     of pipe replacement, the life cycle cost of the system, the operational energy, and the reduction
        33     of GHG emissions, while less attention has been paid to the geographical and socio-economic
        34     issues, along with the areas of human health, water resource diversity, and the hydraulic
        35     characteristics of WDSs.

38   1   Introduction

39   In any modern society, a safe, continuous and adequate drinking water supply should be made
40   available to every individual. Public water utilities and private suppliers obtain water from
41   surface water (lakes, rivers, and sea water desalination) or groundwater sources to meet the
42   demand of consumers in their service areas. The International Energy Agency statistics show that
43   about 590 billion m3 of drinking water is being distributed by the water utilities around the world
44   (IEA 2012). This contributes to nearly 10-15% of the world’s overall water withdrawals
45   (Venkatesh et al. 2014).
46   It is estimated that about 7-8% of the world's total generated electricity is used for drinking water
47   production and distribution systems (Yang et al. 2010; Coelho et al. 2014). Although water
48   resources managers are putting serious effort into water conservation through performance
49   benchmarking of their WSS (Haider et al. 2014), water demand is continually increasing every
50                                         Dr
     year with the growing population (Wakeel et al. 2016). A large portion (~ 65 %) of the total

     electricity in the world is generated by fossil fuel (coal, gas, and oil) power plants, while the
     remainder comes from other sources like hydel, nuclear, and renewable, which contribute about
     10 billion tons of CO2 emissions (IEA 2016; CAMRA 2012). In the USA alone there is about
54   520 million MWh per year of water-related energy use, which produces 295 million tons of CO2
55   emissions per year (Griffiths et al. 2009). Energy generation through fossil fuels contributes to
56   climate change and environmental pollution (Schaum et al. 2015; Stokes et al. 2014; Tabakovic
57   et al. 2012). A substantial part of this energy is being used by water supply systems (WSSs).
58   Water and energy are fundamental natural resources. Fresh water is used for energy production,

 95   2.1    Energy Consumption in Water Distribution Systems
 96   Presently, water utilities aim to operate their WDSs for: i) providing safe water to their
 97   consumers by eliminating contaminants and minimizing the chlorine by-products, ii) maintaining
 98   low operational costs by maximizing energy efficiency and asset management practices, and iii)
 99   minimizing GHG emissions throughout the lifecycle (Sharif et al. 2017; Haider et al. 2014;
100   Bolognesi et al. 2014; Haider et al. 2015). Energy saving in a large WDS depends in large part
101   on process improvement, efficiency targets, and pumping efficiency (Cabrera et al. 2010). Due to
102   high energy prices and the associated GHG emissions, there is an increasing demand to minimize
103   energy requirements and to develop and implement sustainable water use strategies (U.S.
104   Department of Energy 2006).
105   The energy utilization in a WDS depends primarily on energy source, water quality, type of
106   pumping system (continuous ~ intermittent), size of distribution network, the age and network
107   material, type of land use (required pressure head), and the topography of the area (Kenway

               3     Energy Saving Solutions
                                                           aft
      204      3.1    Watergy
      205      “Watergy” is a recent term which was developed with the aim to provide energy saving
      206      solutions, through technical and managerial interventions, for conserving water without
      207      interrupting the desired uses (Barry et al. 2007). The successful implementation of this tool
      208      around the world has revealed immediate improvements in water delivery systems, energy
      209      saving, and water price reductions (AWWA 2003; Barry et al. 2007). The concept of WEN has
      210      not yet been broadly understood or adequately explored through synchronized, holistic efficiency
      211      approaches. The water energy saving approach consists of three main steps, including creating
      212      the political will, technical management, and implementing efficiency measures (Barry et al
      213      2007).
      214      In a WDS, cost-effective benefit analysis improves the energy efficiency and reduces the
      215      payback period. Energy efficiency can be improved in all segments of a distribution network


                                                                                                             7
                                            https://mc06.manuscriptcentral.com/er-pubs
                                               Environmental Reviews                                     Page 8 of 62




216   through regular checking of the pumping systems, energy recovery through in-conduit
217   hydroelectricity, managing leaks, metering and monitoring, automation, and asset management
218   (Barry et al. 2007). A EPA (2013) report states that a water utility in the U.S. can save about
219   $400 million and 5 billion kWh annually through cost-effective energy conservation techniques.
220   Interested readers are refered to Barry et al. (2007) for details.

221   3.2   Optimization and Maintenance of Pumping Systems
222   Recent studies have revealed that most of the operational cost in a WDS is associated with water
223   pumping (Marchi et al. 2012). For example, more than 6-7% of the electricity in California is
224   being used for water pumping systems (CEC 2005). According to the U.S. Department of Energy
225   (2006), about 10 to 30% energy savings can be achieved by optimizing the pump sizes, while the
226   appropriate use of variable speed drives can save an average of 10% of the total electricity
227   required for water distribution. The main obstacles in operating WDSs at high efficiency are
228   their complex networks with large spatial variations, unreliable structure, and time-varying
229   parameters (Swamee 2008). Utilities strive to optimize the operational energy of their WDSs
230
231
                                             Dr
      without compromising on hydraulic performance (i.e., desired flows and pressure heads) and
      system reliability (i.e., minimum interruptions). Preserving the operational security of the
232
233
                                                    aft
      system, by maintaining the hydraulic performance when part of the system is non-operational,
      might lead to higher energy costs (Coelho et al. 2014).
234   It also reported that investing 8 cents/kWh on maintenance of pumping systems can: i) increase
235   pump performance by about 20%; ii) provide savings of 145,100 kWh/year of energy and
236   $11,800 in energy costs; and iii) reduce emissions by around 98 tons CO2 per annum. The three
237   types of pumps (centrifugal, vertical, and submersible) that have been used in WDS depend on
238   network size. The most commonly used type of pump for the delivery of large flows with a
239   stable head is the centrifugal pump (HydraTek 2013). Instead of using a fixed-speed pump
240   (FSP), both the energy use and the GHG emissions can be reduced with the help of a variable
241   speed drive pump (VSP). The optimization of the FSP has been noted in many studies (Wu et al.
242   2012). The VSP is useful in networks where the water demand varies (close pressure zones) and
243   the pump size is not a deciding factor (Wegley et al. 2000; Rao et al. 2007; da Costa et al.
244   2008;Wu et al. 2009). Marchi et al. (2012) evaluated the performance of both the FSP and VSP
245   in a small system (population less than 20,000). They found that the FSP consumed more energy

282   3.3   Leakage Management

283   Leakage in water mains is one of the most significant operational problems in a WDS. Water
284   leakage usually occurs in broken and old buried pipes, water mains, valves and taps. A pressure
285   management can significantly reduce leakage in WDS. Pressure management is more cost-
286   effective than conventional expensive repairs to numerous leaks in buried pipes. Water utilities
287   in Ontario, Canada have saved $2.6 million dollars in energy costs per year by implementing
288   pressure management solutions(Maas 2009). Pressure reduction due to leakage results in a
289   significant loss of embodied energy (CEC 2005). In developing countries, around 30-50 % of
290
291                                        Dr
      urban supplied water is lost due to leaking pipes and system inefficiencies (Barry et al. 2007).
      For example, in large Indian metropolitan cities, about 40% of the water is lost due to leakage,

337   The development of large WDS, without being optimized, cannot fulfill the growing future water
338   demands of local communities (Kiselychnyk et al. 2009). Given the complexities of a WDS,
339   such as the varying sizes of the pipes and the corresponding head losses, including variations in
340   pressure and demand loads across the network, optimization problems can be resolved by using
341   traditional trial and error techniques (Coelho et al. 2014). The use of a nonlinear optimization

343   function or maximizing the benefit (reliability) function of the network subject to equality or
344   inequality constraints, i.e., hydraulic system requirements (Veldhuizen et al. 2007). Various
345   optimization studies have shown that one objective function may be suitable for one system
346   while being inadequate for another system (Berge et al. 2000). No perfect algorithm has been
347   developed so far that is able to solve all of the optimization problems of urban WDS. In the last
348   three decades, the nonlinear (classical and heuristic) optimization approach has been used

      674       4.4.1    Life Cycle Assessment
      675       Life cycle assessment (LCA) is a procedure to estimate the performance of an entire system by
      676       considering a ‘cradle-to-grave’ approach (Nair et al. 2014; SAIC 2006). LCA serves as a useful
      677       information tool to examine future substitution scenarios for strategic planning and decisions
      678       (Sadiq et al. 2006). Assessment of the system starts with the extraction of raw material, followed
      679       by design and manufacturing, eventually culminating in its return to earth (Hendrickson et al.
      680       2014). Figure 11 shows the typical life cycle phases of a WDS from raw material to the end of its
      681       life. LCA has been applied to water technology assessment for nearly three decades (Loubet et
      682       al. 2014).
      683       Most of the past studies on water and wastewater life cycles are related to treatment processes
      684       (Emmerson et al. 1995; Jeong, et al. 2015). Since 2005, a number of LCA studies have
      685       investigated the various life cycle stages of WDS, including pipe manufacturing, pipe repairing
      686       and replacement, environmental factors, water recycling, embodied energy related to water
      687
      688                                              Dr
                leakage, and the operational energy requisite to run a distribution system (Du et al. 2012;
                Herstein et al. 2009; Loubet et al. 2014; MacLeod et al. 2012; Nault et al. 2015). The traditional
      689

                studies of WDS LCAs have focused on the comparison of different pipe materials which
                evaluated the sustainability of WDSs and assessed the future implications of present decisions
      691       (Nault et al. 2015). The sustainability of a WDS can be assessed using a triple-bottom-line
      692       approach which is based on three main dimensions, i.e., economic, social, and the environmental
      693       (Gleick 1994). Due to the aging of infrastructure, an enormous amount of investment for
      694       rehabilitation, replacement, and expansion is required in each step of the life cycle stages of
      695       WDSs (Friedrich et al. 2002; Lundie et al. 2004; Raluy et. al 2005; SAIC 2006; Foley et al.
      696       2010).

                      Summary, Conclusions, and Recommendations

      872       5.1    Summary
      873       All the components of a drinking WSS, including extraction from the source, treatment, and
      874       distribution to the end user, consistently consume energy. Globally, most of the energy is
      875       generated from fossil fuel sources, a part of which is utilized by WSS, resulting in more GHG
      876       emissions, climate change, and environmental pollution. The review of the literature has revealed
      877       that the WEN of drinkable WSS has received more attention in recent years due to an increase in
      878       energy usage and its effect on global warming.
      879       The application of integrated WENs has become more popular due to climate change, population
      880       growth, and increasing energy crises. The above review of the research shows that most of the
      881       existing WDSs around the world were designed when electricity production costs and associated
      882       GHG emissions were not major issues. The energy used for public WDSs signifies that about
      883       25% of the world’s urban energy can be saved through cost effective solutions (CEC 2005).


                                                                                                              29
                                              https://mc06.manuscriptcentral.com/er-pubs
                                              Environmental Reviews                                        Page 30 of 62




884   Most of the studies proposed water-energy saving solutions, like optimizing pumping power and
885   design of the network, and either repairing or replacing pipes through investment.
886   Watergy provides water-energy solutions through technical and managerial changes with little or
887   no investment, such as leakage prevention, and through educating the public to conserve tap
888   water. However, the Watergy application depends on local factors such as topography, water
889   demand, supply, culture, and electricity tariffs. A smaller number of studies report the
890   applications of Watergy solutions in large WDSs.
891   WDS design (pipe diameter) and pump operation (scheduling) optimization have been
892   investigated in the past to decrease both the operational energy cost and the associated
893   environmental pollution. Due to the complex nature of WDS, optimization problems can be
894   solved by using trial and error methods (Coelho et al. 2014). Some studies reported that the use
895   of a nonlinear (classical and heuristic) optimization algorithm is more effective and efficient to
896   improve the design and operation of WDS.
897   Many studies have been conducted on optimal design, minimizing cost, planning, and the
898
899                                         Dr
      operation of the network, while other studies also considered network piping systems (Prosser et
      al. 2013). However, fewer studies measured the real size of the network, the tank size, pump
900
901                                                aft
      scheduling, multiple operating conditions, and the demand variations, simultaneously. Numerous
      studies reported in the literature reveal that pumping energy can be improved using variable-
902   speed-drive pumps (VSPs) rather than fixed-speed-drive pumps (FSPs). It was found in the
903   literature that, after refurbishing the WDS pumps, 11% of the pumps recovered their lost energy
904   efficiency (HydraTek 2013).
905   The energy-perspective LCA models have been reviewed in detail to investigate environmental
906   impacts, GHG emissions, energy used for pump operations, and the life cycle stages of pipe
907   manufacturing (embodied energy) in distribution networks. Some studies considered the LCA of
908   pipe repair or replacement (R & R) and operational optimization, but they did not account for the
909   energy associated with excavation, landfill, pipe transportation, and GHG emissions. However,
910   pipe manufacturing has an embodied energy which is linked with material production.
911   In the last two decades, more attention has been given to renewable energy resources for
912   electricity production with an aim to reduce GHG emissions. Very few studies have reported the
913   use of renewable energy resources, like wind, solar, and micro-hydro turbine, as an alternative

      915       5.2   Conclusions
      916       Numerous studies have revealed that energy efficiency can be improved in all segments of a
      917       WDS by regular checks on the system, higher water pumping efficiency, in-conduit
      918       hydroelectricity, better leak management, infrastructure improvement, and system automation.
      919       Generally, in a large-sized WDS, where a continuous water head is required, fixed-speed pumps
      920       (FSPs) are preferred over variable-speed pumps (VSPs) for the optimization of large WDSs.
      921       VSPs are useful in networks where the water demand varies (closed pressure zones) and the
      922       pump size is not a deciding factor. Most of the reviewed studies suggest a trade-off between
      923       energy cost and the associated GHG emissions when selecting FSPs over VSPs for a large WDS.

      924       Pumping in WDS is done either through direct (pump scheduling) or indirect (storage trigger
      925       level) operations. Most of the past studies on pump scheduling were conducted on fixed-speed
      926       pumps (FSPs). Therefore, the past studies suggested that pumping systems can be optimized by
      927       optimal schedule computation and by optimal pump combinations.

      928       Many researchers have used genetic algorithms (GA) in their studies of pump scheduling
      929
      930
                                                       Dr
                optimization. They found that nature-based algorithms perform well when compared to gradient-
                based ones because they need less computational time. Some studies reviewed above used multi-
      931
      932
                                                             aft
                objective optimization evolutionary algorithms (MOEA) to find the optimal solution to minimize
                energy cost and optimize pump scheduling, power peak time, and water tank levels. However,
      933       these pumps’ operational optimizing methodology cannot be applied to a large complex network
      934       due to their high computational time. A super-efficient supercomputer system is needed to solve
      935       the integrated MOEA simultaneously. Therefore, the advantage of using a combination of
      936       algorithms, rather than the single objective (SO), is reported in the literature.

      937       5.3   Recommendations
      938       The optimization of WDS alone is a challenging task. There are some constraints involved in the
      939       optimization of the network and a trade-off is suggested between hydraulic reliability, the
      940       environment, energy, and the cost of WDS. No perfect algorithm has been developed so far to
      941       solve all of the optimization problems in a large WDS. The optimal design of WDS still needs
      942       further exploration to cover all multi-objective parameters of a real and complex network.
      943       Very few studies of pump optimization have considered GHG emissions with the pumping cost
      944       and operational energy of the system. Therefore, further research on a multi-objective (min. cost,


                                                                                                               31
                                              https://mc06.manuscriptcentral.com/er-pubs
                                             Environmental Reviews                                        Page 32 of 62




945   min GHG, and max. hydraulic benefits) optimization study for large-sized networks is still
946   required.
947   The embodied energy associated with pipe manufacturing consumes more energy and produces
948   more GHG emissions. Pipe repair is more cost effective than replacement and has fewer GHG
949   emissions. However, the reviewed studies suggest a trade-off between pipe replacement and
950   embodied energy. This review of the previous research shows that the embodied energy related
951   to the water distribution infrastructure has not been adequately addressed so far.
952   The reviewed case studies showed that, among all of the conducted studies, the micro-hydro
953   turbine (used in water mains) is the most feasible and cost effective, while also having less
954   associated GHG emissions. However, the capital cost, initial investment and payback period for
955   all these renewable energy systems are still relatively high. Therefore, it is recommended that
956   future research should not only focus on cost, operational energy, and the optimized design of
957   WDS, but also on the sources of electricity generation (renewable energy) in order to reduce
958   GHG emissions.
